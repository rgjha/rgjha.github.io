01

How to read the picture

Every image on this page is a map of energy density in space and time. Position runs left-to-right. Time runs bottom-to-top: the bottom edge is the start of the experiment, and as your eye travels up, the clock ticks forward. Color shows how much energy sits at each place and moment, measured relative to the empty vacuum — blank means vacuum, bright means a particle is there.

A particle moving at constant velocity traces a straight slanted line — a worldline. Two particles fired toward each other from opposite sides make two lines that meet: an X. What happens at and after the crossing is the entire story of the collision, and different collisions draw visibly different X's.

Why this is unusual

The textbook way to think about scattering compares the particles long before and long after they meet — the asymptotic "in" and "out" states — and never looks in between. Here we get to watch the middle: the moment of contact, resolved in space and time. As Preskill put it, "seeing processes unfold in real time provides an edifying perspective."

02

One theory, one knob

The playground is Ising Field Theory (IFT) — the continuum theory you get by sitting the famous two-dimensional Ising model right at its critical point and then nudging it with two perturbations: a "thermal" one that moves the temperature off critical, and a "magnetic" one, an external field. Their relative strength is captured by a single dimensionless number,

$$\eta \;=\; \frac{\tau}{|h|^{8/15}}$$
(1)

and turning that one knob sweeps through a whole family of theories.

The two ends are special and, in different ways, easy. At $\eta = \infty$ (pure thermal) IFT is just a free massive fermion: particles pass through each other and nothing happens. At $\eta = 0$ (pure magnetic) lies one of the jewels of mathematical physics — Zamolodchikov's $E_8$ theory, an integrable theory with eight species of stable particle whose mass ratios are fixed by the $E_8$ Lie algebra. The lightest two are in the golden ratio: $m_2/m_1 = \tfrac{1+\sqrt5}{2} \approx 1.618$. (This spectrum isn't a fantasy — it has been seen in neutron scattering off quasi-1D Ising magnets.)

Everywhere between those ends, IFT is non-integrable and strongly coupled — the hard, interesting regime, and the one this work targets. As $\eta$ grows the theory even sheds particles: three stable species near $E_8$, then two, then one.

03

Building a collider out of a spin chain

IFT can be grown from something very concrete: a long chain of quantum spins with a simple, tunable interaction. Tuned near its critical point, the chain's low-energy behavior is Ising Field Theory. So a scattering experiment becomes a numerical experiment on a spin chain, in three moves.

  • Write the state as a matrix product state (MPS). A one-dimensional state that isn't too entangled can be captured compactly by a chain of small matrices. That compression is what makes any of this tractable — and gapped particles are exactly this kind of state.
  • Build wave packets and aim them. On top of the vacuum, insert two localized lumps — particles — with opposite momenta, wide and well-separated, so they start far apart and head toward each other. Real packets span ~70–120 sites in a window of 1000–2000.
  • Let time run, with TDVP. The time-dependent variational principle evolves the MPS forward, keeping it inside the compressible family at each step. Record the energy density at every frame — that record is the space-time picture.

The method has a natural ceiling: as particles collide they generate entanglement, the matrices must grow, and eventually the compression stops paying off. But up to that point you get the real-time evolution of a strongly coupled field theory in full spatial and temporal detail — something diagrammatic methods simply cannot deliver.

04

Three things a collision can do

Dial up the collision energy and the character of the X changes. The explorer below is the heart of it: set the energy and the coupling, and watch the space-time picture — and the numbers — respond.

Process 1

Elastic — a bounce with a delay

At low energy the only thing that can happen is an elastic bounce: two particles in, the same two out. But the collision still leaves a fingerprint. The outgoing tracks are shifted in time relative to a naïve straight-through crossing — a time delay, first studied by Wigner. Its size is set by how the scattering phase changes with energy,

$$\Delta t \;=\; -i\,\partial_E \log S_{11\to11}(E,\eta)$$
(2)

so measuring the delay across energies reconstructs the phase of the S-matrix — the thing that actually characterizes the interaction. Near the integrable ends the extracted delays agree with form-factor perturbation theory; in between, the simulation goes where no formula reaches.

Process 2

Resonance — a particle that almost exists

Near the $E_8$ point some of the heavy particles sit just above the two-particle threshold. Away from integrability they are no longer quite stable: they become resonances, short-lived states that form and then fall apart. Tune the collision energy onto one and the space-time picture shows it directly — a bright lump of energy parks itself at the collision point and fades, and from the fade rate you read the resonance's lifetime, hence its width $\Gamma$ and mass. The paper pins down the mass $m_4$ and width $\Gamma_4$ of the lightest resonance as $\eta$ varies.

In Preskill's words "A lump of energy density appears, which gradually dissipates as the resonance decays to outgoing particles. By fitting the decay rate of the lump, we can determine the lifetime of the resonance."

Process 3

Inelastic — making new particles

Push the energy high enough and the collision can do what colliders are built for: convert energy into new particles. Above threshold, the outgoing X sprouts extra tracks. Here the quantum strangeness is on full display — the outgoing state is a superposition of every allowed final channel at once, all the different possible sets of product particles overlaid in the same picture. To disentangle them, the authors project the wavefunction onto definite particle sectors or read energy-density correlations.

In Preskill's words "The outgoing tracks capture all of the possible final-state channels, which appear in superposition. Energy density correlation functions clarify the interpretation of the final state."

05

The question at the top of the energy scale

Here is a question that sounds simple and isn't. Send two particles in at higher and higher energy and ask: what fraction of the time do they just bounce (elastic), rather than shatter into more particles (inelastic)? Call that high-energy limit

$$P^{\infty}(\eta) \;=\; \lim_{E\to\infty} P_{11\to11}(E,\eta).$$
(3)

Is it zero (production wins), one (bouncing wins), or something in between? Both integrable ends are known to give $P^{\infty} = 1$. For the strongly-coupled middle, nobody could say. Pushing the simulations to several times the production threshold, the results line up with a striking conjecture of Zamolodchikov: the answer is a sharp switch — production wins completely below some critical coupling $\eta_c$, and bouncing wins completely above it.

A walk through the pictures, from a co-author

When the paper appeared, John Preskill narrated the figures on X. His thread is the cleanest short guide to what the movies show — reproduced here.

John Preskill @preskill Nov 22, 2024 · view thread
  1. I am very proud of our new paper on simulations of scattering processes in (1+1)-dimensional quantum field theory. All the hard work was done by my co-authors Raghav Jha, Ash Milsted, Dominik Neuenfeld, and Pedro Vieira.
  2. Using tensor network methods, we studied wave-packet collisions in Ising field theory. These pictures depict various processes that occur. Time is on the vertical axis and spatial position is on the horizontal axis. The colors show the energy density relative to the vacuum state.
  3. The picture on the left shows an elastic scattering event. The particles emerge from the scattering region with a time delay that reveals information about how the scattering phase shift depends on the energy.
  4. The picture in the middle shows elastic scattering with total energy close to the mass of a narrow resonance. A lump of energy density appears, which gradually dissipates as the resonance decays to outgoing particles.
  5. By fitting the decay rate of the lump, we can determine the lifetime of the resonance. (Pairs of tracks are seen propagating outward in each direction due to a subtle interference effect that we explain in the paper.)
  6. The picture on the right shows a process in which both elastic and inelastic scattering occur. The outgoing tracks capture all of the possible final-state channels, which appear in superposition. Energy density correlation functions clarify the interpretation of the final state.
  7. Seeing processes unfold in real time provides an edifying perspective, complementing the more conventional approach to scattering, which focuses on the relation between asymptotic in and out states rather than the dynamical evolution while the particles are interacting.
  8. Aside from generating pretty pictures, we obtain quantitative results that characterize the S-matrix of the theory. It would be instructive to do the same for other field theories in one dimension and beyond.
  9. Someday (I can't say when), we'll be able to study more complex processes at higher energy using quantum computers!
